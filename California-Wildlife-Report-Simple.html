California Animal Collision Report 2024

Complete Information About Animals Hit by Cars on California Roads

Information from: UC Davis University | California Transportation Department | Highway Patrol

The Big Picture

Every year, tens of thousands of wild animals are hit and killed by cars on California roads. This report shows where these accidents happen most, which animals are affected, and how much damage it causes.

Key Numbers You Should Know:

48,000+ animals killed every year on California roads

$1-2 Billion in costs over 5 years (2018-2023)

25 most dangerous spots identified

15,000+ miles of highways monitored

What Does This Mean?

  • Wild animals are dying on our roads at an alarming rate
  • Some roads are far more dangerous than others
  • Certain animals like mountain lions and bears are being threatened
  • We can fix this problem if we work on it

Why is Interstate 280 the worst?

Interstate 280 in the San Francisco Bay Area is the deadliest road for animals. It costs about $178,000 per mile every year in animal collision damage. This highway cuts through important animal habitats, and thousands of small frogs called newts try to cross during certain times of year.

The Alma Bridge Road Surprise

A small road called Alma Bridge Road in San Jose has something unusual - it has the world's highest number of frog and newt deaths! About 5,000 small frogs die there every year trying to reach their breeding areas. This is more frog deaths than anywhere else on Earth.

What Does This Cost Mean?

The $1-2 billion includes:

  • Damage to cars (about $4,000-$8,000 per accident)
  • Medical help for injured people
  • Police and emergency services
  • Removing dead animals from roads
  • Loss of wildlife for our environment

Important to Know: These numbers are ONLY accidents that were reported. Many more accidents happen but nobody reports them. The real number of animals hit is probably 50% to 100% more than these numbers.

Why Should We Care About Each Animal?

🦌 Deer (48,000+ killed)

Deer are hit the most. Many are killed in spring (when they have babies) and fall (when they look for food). Some areas are losing so many deer that the population may not recover.

🐸 Newts (22,000+ killed)

These are small frogs. Every year, thousands try to cross roads to lay eggs in water. Alma Bridge Road is where most die - it's the worst place on Earth for frog deaths.

🐻 Black Bears (557 killed)

Bears need huge areas to live. When they're hit by cars, California loses some of these important animals. Most are hit in the mountains during spring and fall.

🦁 Mountain Lions (300+ killed)

These big cats are endangered. There aren't many left, and each one that dies by car is a big loss. Cities are growing into their habitat, forcing them to cross dangerous roads.

🐺 Coyotes (2,000+ killed)

Coyotes are adapting to live around people. But they still get hit by cars, especially in Los Angeles and the Bay Area. Their population is doing okay despite the deaths.

Animals Being Hit in Different Parts of California

🌉 Bay Area (San Francisco & Surrounding)

The Bay Area has the worst problem with animal collisions.

  • Interstate 280: The absolute worst - has 5,300+ hits per year
  • Alma Bridge Road: 5,000+ frogs and newts per year
  • Highway 17: Lots of deer and mountain lions - 200+ per year
  • Highway 101 in Marin: 150+ hits per year

Main Problem: Too many cities taking over animal habitats. Highways cutting through where animals need to go.

⛰️ Sierra Nevada (Mountains)

Multiple mountain highways have serious animal collision problems.

  • Highway 88, 4, 108, 50, 49: All mountain passes with 50-120 hits per year
  • Main Animals: Deer and black bears
  • When Worst: Spring (animals leaving winter areas) and fall (looking for food)

Main Problem: These mountains are the only way for animals to move. Roads cut across their natural paths.

🌲 Northern California (Far North)

Remote areas with lots of forests and wildlife.

  • Interstate 5, Highway 20, 44, 299: Have 40-90 hits per year
  • Main Animals: Bears, deer, sometimes mountain lions

Main Problem: Fewer alternative roads, so highways are only routes for animals.

🏙️ Southern California (Los Angeles & San Diego)

Huge cities with lots of highways and animals squeezed into small areas.

  • Interstate 405 (LA): 80-120 hits per year (mostly coyotes and deer)
  • Highway 2 (LA Mountains): 70-100 hits per year (mountain lions and deer)
  • Interstate 15 & 8 (San Diego): 60-90 hits per year

Main Problem: Cities and suburbs taking over all the land. Animals have nowhere to go but across highways.

🌊 Central Coast (Coastal Areas)

Beautiful coastal roads with less traffic but still animal collisions.

  • Highway 101 (Santa Barbara & Monterey): 40-70 hits per year
  • Highway 1 (Big Sur): 30-50 hits per year on scenic coast

Main Problem: Wildlife corridors being broken up. Remote areas harder to help.

Data Limitations

  • Many small animal deaths are never found or reported
  • Accidents that don't hurt people often go unreported
  • Accidents in remote areas might not be discovered
  • Some roads have better reporting than others
  • Weather affects how many animals are on roads

What Can We Do to Fix This Problem?

Scientists and experts have found solutions that actually work. Here's what helps:

Wildlife Overpasses and Underpasses

Special bridges and tunnels let animals cross highways safely without crossing traffic. These work really well!

  • Reduce animal deaths by 90% or more
  • Cost: $500,000 to $5,000,000 each
  • Pay for themselves in 2-5 years by preventing accidents
  • Being built on Highway 17 and Highway 2 now

Fencing

Fences guide animals away from dangerous parts of roads and toward safe crossing points.

  • Works best with bridges or underpasses
  • Stop animals from crossing randomly
  • Keep animals moving toward safe areas

Habitat Connection

Protecting natural areas on both sides of roads so animals don't need to cross as much.

  • Restore forests and streams
  • Create wildlife corridors
  • Prevent more cities from taking over animal land

Slower Speeds in Danger Zones

Reducing speed limits in the worst areas helps animals escape and reduces accident severity.

  • Studies show 20% reduction in animal deaths with lower speeds
  • Gives drivers more time to stop
  • Gives animals more time to escape

Warning Signs

Signs telling drivers about animal danger zones.

  • Reminds drivers to watch for animals
  • Some use electronic signs that light up when animals are present
  • Helps but less effective than other solutions

Seasonal Closures or Restrictions

Some roads could have special rules during peak animal movement times.

  • Spring and fall have the most animal crossings
  • Might close certain lanes or reduce traffic during migration
  • Protects animals and prevents accidents

Continue Research and Monitoring

Keep tracking animal movements and testing solutions.

  • Use GPS collars on animals to understand movement patterns
  • Monitor how well wildlife crossings are working
  • Share findings with other states and countries
