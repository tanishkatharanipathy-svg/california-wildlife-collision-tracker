Key Findings

  • Interstate 280 (Bay Area) is the deadliest highway with $178,439/mile/year in wildlife collision costs
  • Alma Bridge Road (Santa Clara County) has the world's highest amphibian roadkill rate: 5,000+ Pacific newts per year

  • Black bear collisions: 557 killed in 5 years
  • Mule deer remains the primary species involved with 48,000+ killed annually
  • Actual collision numbers are likely much higher due to underreporting of incidents without human injury

Species-Specific Concerns

🦌 Mule Deer (48,000+ killed)

  • Most frequently involved species in all regions
  • Peak collision seasons: Spring migration (March-May) and Fall rutting (September-November)
  • Note: Declining populations in some areas, suggesting the current roadkill rate may not be sustainable

🐸 Pacific Newts (22,000+ killed)

  • Unprecedented amphibian roadkill concentration on Alma Bridge Road
  • Mass migration events to breeding grounds trigger annual mortality spikes
  • World's highest documented amphibian road mortality rate
  • Critical concern: Population decline trajectory unknown but likely severe

🐻 Black Bears (557 killed)

  • Concentrated in Sierra Nevada and Northern California
  • Highways 88, 108, 50 are major conflict zones
  • Seasonal peaks during spring emergence and fall foraging

🦁 Mountain Lions (300+ killed)

  • Conservation Priority: Small California population makes each loss significant
  • Critical habitat fragmentation from roads (especially Highway 17, Highway 2)
  • Urban expansion limiting genetic diversity and population recovery

🐺 Coyotes (2,000+ killed)

  • Urban adaptation indicates tolerance to human landscapes
  • Southern California and Bay Area consistently high
  • Population remains stable despite annual losses

Data Collection Methodology

Primary Collection Methods

  1. California Highway Patrol Reports: Officers respond to major collisions and hazardous conditions
  2. Citizen Observations (CROS): Public reports of observed roadkill
  3. Agency Wildlife Removal: Caltrans, CalTrans employees, wildlife officers
  4. Automated Detection: Some areas use traffic cameras and sensors
  5. Researcher Field Surveys: UC Davis and partner organizations conduct systematic surveys

Quality Assurance

  • Species identification verified by experts (>97% accuracy CROS)
  • GPS coordinates verified against geographic database
  • Temporal validation (date/time checks)
  • Duplicate detection and removal
  • Cross-referencing between CHP reports and CROS observations

Data Limitations & Considerations

⚠️ Underreporting: Actual collision numbers likely 50-100% higher than reported. Many collisions go unreported, especially those without:

  • Human injury
  • Major property damage
  • Police involvement
  • Visible remains
  • Geographic Bias: Higher reporting rates in urbanized areas near highways vs. remote regions
  • Seasonal Variation: Spring/fall peak periods may skew annual averages
  • Weather Effects: Weather-related closures affect collision opportunity and reporting
  • Population Changes: Declining wildlife populations may mask true collision rates
  • Vehicle Type Bias: Small animal collisions rarely reported vs. large animal collisions always reported

Mitigation & Solutions

Implemented/Planned Wildlife Crossings

  • Highway 17 (Santa Cruz Mountains): Major wildlife crossing infrastructure projects underway
  • Interstate 405 (Los Angeles): Coyote and deer crossing considerations in highway improvements
  • Highway 2 (San Gabriel Mountains): Mountain lion crossing projects proposed
  • Alma Bridge Road (Santa Clara): Amphibian crossing and habitat restoration projects

Recommended Mitigation Strategies

Infrastructure Solutions

  • Wildlife overpasses and underpasses (proven 90%+ effectiveness)
  • Fencing to direct wildlife to crossing structures
  • Riparian habitat restoration for corridor connectivity
  • Road design modifications to reduce collision zones

Operational Measures

  • Speed limit reductions in critical hotspots (studies show 20% reduction in fatalities)
  • Seasonal closures or restrictions during migration peaks
  • Warning signage and dynamic message systems
  • Timed traffic signals coordinated with wildlife movement

Research & Monitoring

  • Continued CROS data collection and analysis
  • GPS collar studies on animal movement patterns
  • Population monitoring to assess road impacts
  • Crossing structure effectiveness monitoring

Policy & Management

  • Integrate wildlife-vehicle conflict into transportation planning
  • Establish regional wildlife corridors protected from fragmentation
  • Funding for conservation and crossing projects
  • Coordination between agencies (Caltrans, CHP, CDFW, local jurisdictions)
